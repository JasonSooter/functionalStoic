# Pruitt-Igoe

Public housing complex on the north side of [[St Louis|St. Louis]], demolished from 1972. James Clear and Charles Montgomery both use it as the emblem of modernist housing's failure; in _Happy City_ it shows how design shapes how neighbours behave.

## At a glance
| Source | Author | Role | In one line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Why Old Ideas Are a Secret Weapon | James Clear | subject | Modernist tower blocks opened in 1954 and blown up in 1972, the essay's example of new ideas failing. |

## Why Old Ideas Are a Secret Weapon — James Clear (2015)
Source: [Original](https://jamesclear.com/old-ideas) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5gby0kyzy15jdpxrqmc9eca)

> Spanning 57 acres across the north side of St. Louis, Pruitt-Igoe consisted of 33 high-rise buildings and provided nearly 3,000 new apartments to the surrounding population.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the article:** The 33 high-rise towers, nearly 3,000 apartments on 57 acres, opened in 1954 with features such as skip-stop elevators and unbreakable lights that failed in practice; less than twenty years later the complex was demolished.
- **Why it matters:** It is the essay's opening case for trusting ideas that have survived the test of time.

## Happy City — Charles Montgomery (2013)
Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL21093281W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m83qjksdnwsa6d4svxr3k9e9)

> The most spectacular and symbolic failure was Minoru Yamasaki’s thirty-three-block Pruitt-Igoe housing complex, built in 1950s St. Louis.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the book:** Yamasaki's identical blocks stood in lawns nobody used. Architect Oscar Newman found that landings shared by a couple of families were kept up, while corridors shared by twenty families and lobbies shared by 150 became disasters, "indefensible space"; demolition began in 1972, while a nearby row-house project fared well. Montgomery adds that poverty and poor management also played their part.
- **Why it matters:** It is the book's starkest case that the number of people sharing a space shapes whether neighbours look after it and each other.
