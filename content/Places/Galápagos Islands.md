## Early Retirement from the Sale of a Multi-Million Dollar Business with Steve Miller — Steve Miller (2015)
Source: [Original](https://www.themoneymine.com/early-retirement-from-the-sale-of-a-multi-million-dollar-business-with-steve-miller/) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5hj180j2rs2jj82ssrhd6gr)

> We then made our way to the Galapagos Islands, Machu Picchu and the Bahamas. We saw extraordinary things. We watched a tortoise painstakingly dig a hole for her eggs.

- **Role:** journey waypoint
- **In the article:** After selling his business, Miller and his wife begin travelling; the Galapagos come first in a run that also takes in Machu Picchu and the Bahamas. The scene he picks out is a tortoise slowly digging a hole for her eggs.
- **Why it matters:** It stands for what the sale bought him: time to watch something slow and extraordinary. A minor stop in a long list of trips.

## Life on Air — David Attenborough (2002)
Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL24547776M/Life_on_air) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5hy950ykq7aavr7pm012gxb)

> And in May 2006 Miles and I were sitting surrounded by marine iguanas on the Galapagos Islands.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** Opening Life in Cold Blood on his eightieth birthday, the team films marine iguanas basking on lava rocks with a thermal camera showing them warming from blue to yellow.
- **Why it matters:** Opening location of the final series; thermal camera demonstrates cold-blooded thermoregulation.

## A Short History of Nearly Everything — Bill Bryson (2003)

Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL74128W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m833150prmg45wj1s5gt66fr)

> found teeming colonies of large organisms living on and around deep-sea vents off the Galápagos Islands—tube worms over ten feet long, clams a foot wide, shrimps and mussels in profusion, wriggling spaghetti worms.

> Nor, above all, were his conclusions in any way inspired by his noticing, during his time in the Galápagos Islands, an interesting diversity in the beaks of finches.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** In 1977 the submersible _Alvin_ finds teeming colonies around deep-sea vents off the Galápagos: tube worms over ten feet long, foot-wide clams, shrimps and mussels, all living on bacteria that feed on hydrogen sulphide. Bryson calls it one of the most important biological discoveries of the century and notes that the vents also help keep the seas from growing saltier. Later, in the Darwin chapter, he debunks the story that finch beaks seen here inspired natural selection: it was John Gould who realised the birds were all finches, and Darwin, still an inexperienced naturalist, had not even noted which island each came from.
- **Why it matters:** It overturns the idea that all life depends on sunlight, a turning point for the book's story of life; the finch passage shows Bryson correcting a popular myth about how Darwin's idea arose.
