# Schwabentor

Medieval city gate on the east side of Freiburg's old town, towards the Dreisam, Germany, where a First World War "nail tree" for the Red Cross once stood. See [[Freiburg|Freiburg]].

## VAG History: Freiburg Tram Drivers Also Collected for the Bereaved and Refugees During the First World War — VAG Freiburg (2026)
Source: [Original](https://blog.vag-freiburg.de/vag-historisch-auch-freiburger-strassenbahnfahrer-sammelten-im-ersten-weltkrieg-fuer-hinterbliebene-und-fluechtlinge/) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5beybyscfn1v6v2m955zgx2)

> As the location, they chose the space between the two gate passages at the Schwabentor towards the Dreisam.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the article:** In late 1915 the town council set up a felled linden tree, four metres high, under a shelter at the gate. Citizens bought iron, silver or golden nails (one, three or ten marks) to drive into it, with the money going to the local Red Cross for the wounded, bereaved and refugees; tram staff donated too. The tree stayed after the war, went to the Natural History Museum in 1931 because of weathering, was re-erected at the gate on the new Nazi mayor's order in 1933, decayed without a roof, moved to the cloister of the Augustiner Museum in 1936, and was burned in 1938.
- **Why it matters:** The gate is the monument's home for most of its life; the article traces the city's politics through where the tree was allowed to stand.

## Trams in Freiburg im Breisgau - Wikipedia — Wikipedia (2026)
Source: [[Trams in Freiburg Im Breisgau - Wikipedia]] · [Original](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Freiburg_im_Breisgau) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m7h2cafx70vsqz8jzs7v52dy)

> At the end of the 19th century, there were talks about demolishing the Schwabentor, but it was expanded instead. After World War II, it was returned to its original state.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the article:** Two of the early lines could only open once the gate was converted, with a second passage added for the double track, as at the Martinstor. The article notes that the trams pass through three historic gates (Schwabentor, Martinstor and Günterstäler Tor), something only two other places in the German-speaking world share (Potsdam and Bern).
- **Why it matters:** It is the network's most distinctive quirk: trams running through a medieval gate that was rebuilt for them instead of being torn down.
