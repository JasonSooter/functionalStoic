# Tower of London

Medieval fortress on the Thames in [[London]]. In _A Short History of Nearly Everything_ it is the starting point of Richard Norwood's two-year march to York to measure a degree of the Earth.

## A Short History of Nearly Everything — Bill Bryson (2003)

Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL74128W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m80ghf5skr7k7nt4fc1z21ee)

> Starting with his back against the Tower of London, Norwood spent two devoted years marching 208 miles north to York, repeatedly stretching and measuring a length of chain as he went

- **Role:** journey waypoint
- **In the book:** In the chapter on measuring the Earth, the mathematician Richard Norwood starts from the Tower and spends two years chaining his way north to York, then compares the Sun's angle there with his London reading. Bryson reports that his degree of arc came out accurate to within about 600 yards of the true figure.
- **Why it matters:** The Tower is a fixed, famous baseline for one of the earliest serious measurements of the planet's size; any known start point would have served, but it makes the scale of the walk concrete.
