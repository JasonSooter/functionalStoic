# Bogotá

Capital of [[Colombia]], about 8,660 feet above sea level. In _Happy City_ it is Charles Montgomery's model of a city remade for happiness; in a Reasons to be Cheerful article it is where migrant couriers ride e-bikes up its hills.

## For Delivery Workers in Latin America, Affordable E-Bikes Are a Superpower — Mariel Lozada (2025)
Source: [Original](https://reasonstobecheerful.world/e-bike-boom-delivery-workers/) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5ebqzszgg4mda26kwkpy6sd)

> Nuñez moved to Bogotá and started working with delivery apps a year ago. On a regular bike, the hills of the city were a struggle: Bogotá sits 8,660 feet above sea level, one of the highest capitals in the world.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the article:** Venezuelan migrants like Maldonado and Nuñez work as Rappi couriers in Bogotá, where motorbikes dominate and altitude and hills make pedal bikes hard; e-bikes let Nuñez climb any hill and earn more.
- **Why it matters:** The city's altitude and motorbike culture are the concrete problem the e-bikes solve.

## Happy City — Charles Montgomery (2013)

Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL21093281W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m83dc0sbytkzq3dd41ehf9sf)

> Toward the end of the twentieth century, Bogotá had become a truly horrible place to live—one of the very worst on earth.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** The opening chapter: Montgomery chases former mayor Enrique Peñalosa by bicycle across the north of the city on a car-free day in 2007, as Peñalosa campaigns to return to office. The book recounts how Bogotá, once among the worst places on earth to live, dropped a highway plan under Peñalosa for bike paths, parks, plazas, libraries and a bus rapid transit system. On the first car-free day, February 24, 2000, more than eight hundred thousand vehicles stayed off the streets and nobody was killed in traffic. Chapter 10, "Who Is the City For?", returns in detail: in 1995 the city recorded 3,363 murders and three-quarters of residents expected life to get worse. Mayor Antanas Mockus worked on civic behaviour (mimes, red cards, melted-down guns), then Peñalosa rebuilt the "hardware": he scrapped a plan for elevated highways, put TransMilenio buses on the best road space, planted bollards to free sidewalks from parked cars, and built parks, schools and monumental libraries in poor districts such as El Paraíso. Merchants tried to have him impeached over his "war on cars", but by the end of his term his approval rating hit 80 percent and accident and murder rates had fallen by nearly half. The chapter ends by noting that crowding on TransMilenio and fading optimism show such gains need sustained public investment.
- **Why it matters:** Bogotá is the book's founding proof of concept, the claim that even a poor, troubled city can be redesigned for happiness, and in chapter 10 its main case that fairness in how a city shares space is itself a source of well-being.
