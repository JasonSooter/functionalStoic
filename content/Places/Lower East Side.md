# Lower East Side

Lower East Side is a historically Jewish neighborhood in Manhattan. It turns up in Four Thousand Weeks (Oliver Burkeman), Quiet (Susan Cain) and How to Know a Person (David Brooks), where it is the stretch where five generations of Brooks's family lived.

## Four Thousand Weeks — Oliver Burkeman (2021)
Source: [[Four Thousand Weeks]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL22355777W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5fcqky32gk5b569xkdg3x3r)

> Friends of mine live in an apartment building on the historically Jewish Lower East Side

- **Role:** backdrop
- **In the book:** Friends of the author live in a building here with a Shabbat elevator that stops at every floor from Friday evening to Saturday night, so Jewish residents need not operate a switch.
- **Why it matters:** A minor illustration of how religious rules make rest happen. Little depends on the location beyond its Jewish heritage.

## Quiet — Susan Cain (2012)
Source: [[Quiet]] · [Original](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/55983/quiet-by-cain-susan/9780141970172) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5g8wjjwqef3detjqsgcz6k9)

> As a volunteer at a settlement house on Manhattan’s impoverished Lower East Side, she had met children who were forced to sew artificial flowers in windowless factories to the point of exhaustion.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** As a young volunteer at a settlement house on the Lower East Side, Eleanor met children forced to make artificial flowers in windowless factories. She took Franklin there, he saw how the poor lived for the first time, and he soon fell in love with her.
- **Why it matters:** The visit shows Eleanor's conscience and starts the partnership the chapter is about.

## How to Know a Person — David Brooks (2023)
Source: [[How to Know a Person]] · [Original](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/652822/how-to-know-a-person-by-david-brooks/) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m8gxrbr5qeczqewjafxtdxm1)

> I grew up in Manhattan. If you start around Fourteenth Street on the East Side and walk south for a mile or so, you’ll pass where my great-grandfather had his butcher shop, where my grandfather worked at a law firm, where my father, on the other side of my family, grew up, where I went to elementary school, and where my son, for a time, went to college.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the book:** In the chapter on how ancestors show up in a life, right after Hurston's Eatonville, Brooks turns to his own home place: five generations of his family in one mile of the East Side. He lists the playground where a dog attacked him, a deli counter, Lafayette Street where the hippies hung out, and a spot on Second Avenue where his grandfather took him for pancakes. "First I inhabited New York, and forever after it inhabits me."
- **Why it matters:** It is Brooks's personal proof of his claim that the place you come from never leaves you. He says he may never live in New York again but can never fully live anywhere else.
- **Real vs. fictional:** Brooks never names the neighbourhood. The mile south of Fourteenth Street on the East Side runs through the East Village into the Lower East Side, so the section sits here.
