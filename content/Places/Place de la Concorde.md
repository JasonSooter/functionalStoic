# Place de la Concorde

Paris's largest square, at the foot of the Champs-Élysées. Tom Hanks's 'A Junket in the City of Light' ends with Rory crossing it; in _A Short History of Nearly Everything_, as the Place de la Révolution, it is where Lavoisier was guillotined.

## Uncommon Type — Tom Hanks (2017)
Source: [[Uncommon Type]] · [Original](https://penguin.co.uk/books/1114786/uncommon-type/9781786091338.html) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5k5v3qpv3sxghx8dz0pfxa7)

> Beyond the gardens was a big traffic circle called Place de la Concorde—millions of cars and Vespa scooters, lanes and lanes of them going both ways and around and around a monument needle in the center. A huge Ferris wheel has been there since 1999.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** With the tour canceled, Rory walks out alone, crossing the river, passing Notre Dame and the Louvre pyramid, then the Tuileries where crews are dismantling the stage for the canceled event. At the Place de la Concorde he buys a ticket and rides the nearly empty Ferris wheel several times, seeing the whole city.
- **Why it matters:** The ride closes the story: Rory, unrecognized and no longer a promoted commodity, finally sees Paris for himself and takes it in 'for free'.

## A Short History of Nearly Everything — Bill Bryson (2003)

Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL74128W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m80gjbs4t0670xk0gq31kx5p)

> Lavoisier and the others were taken directly to the Place de la Revolution (now the Place de la Concorde), site of the busiest of French guillotines. Lavoisier watched his father-in-law beheaded, then stepped up and accepted his fate.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** After the Revolutionary Tribunal condemns the farmers-general in 1794, Lavoisier is taken straight to the guillotine in what was then the Place de la Révolution. Bryson adds that Robespierre was executed there less than three months later, ending the Terror.
- **Why it matters:** The square is where chemistry loses its founder to politics; Bryson uses the execution to close his portrait of Lavoisier.
