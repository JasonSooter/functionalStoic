# Gulf Stream

Warm Atlantic current that runs from the Gulf of Mexico through the Straits of Florida, just off Cuba's north coast. In The Old Man and the Sea it is the sea Santiago fishes, where the marlin tows him for three days.

|---|---|---|---|

## The Old Man and the Sea — Ernest Hemingway (1952)
Source: [[The Old Man and the Sea]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL7265161M) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5gm03hcyhs5kjnxyh9fbfaf)

> He saw the phosphorescence of the Gulf weed in the water as he rowed over the part of the ocean that the fishermen called the great well because there was a sudden deep of seven hundred fathoms

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** Santiago rows far out over the part of the ocean the fishermen call the great well, where the current swirls against a steep floor seven hundred fathoms down. Hooked there, the marlin tows the skiff north-west and then east for three days and nights before Santiago kills it and sails home.
- **Why it matters:** Nearly the whole novella happens on this water; the current's pull is what carries Santiago out of sight of land and makes the return impossible to defend against the sharks.

## A Short History of Nearly Everything — Bill Bryson (2003)

Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL74128W)

> Every day, the Gulf Stream carries an amount of heat to Europe equivalent to the world’s output of coal for ten years, which is why Britain and Ireland have such mild winters compared with Canada and Russia.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the book:** Every day the Gulf Stream carries heat to Europe equivalent to the world's coal output for ten years, which is why Britain and Ireland have mild winters compared with Canada and Russia. Bryson explains that the Atlantic's salt makes surface water sink and drive a slow deep current south; without it, the currents would run up to the Arctic and Europe would lose its warmth.
- **Why it matters:** It introduces thermohaline circulation, the ocean's role in the climate.
