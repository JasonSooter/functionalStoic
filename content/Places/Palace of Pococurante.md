# Palace of Pococurante

An invented villa of a Venetian senator on the Brenta, the river lined with patricians' summer villas between Venice and Padua. In _Candide_ it is the house of a man who owns everything and enjoys nothing.

## Candide — Voltaire (1759)

Source: [[Candide]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL100672W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5m75ph6d8pm54fb0fbnsazm)

> Candide and Martin went in a gondola on the Brenta, and arrived at the palace of the noble Signor Pococurante. The gardens, laid out with taste, were adorned with fine marble statues.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** Candide and Martin go by gondola up the Brenta to visit the Senator, said never to have felt uneasiness. Pococurante dismisses his Raphaels, his concert, Homer, Virgil, Horace, Cicero, Milton and his library, and calls his garden in bad taste. Candide thinks him the happiest of men; Martin sees that he is disgusted with all he has.
- **Why it matters:** The book's chapter on jaded taste: wealth and culture do not bring happiness. A villa on the Brenta was the natural home for a rich Venetian patrician.
- **Where is it?:** ⚠️ The palace and its owner are invented. The text says only "that fine palace on the Brenta" and that they "went in a gondola on the Brenta" from Venice. I found no reference work tying it to a real villa. Pinned at the Riviera del Brenta, the stretch of the river between Venice and Padua lined with Venetian villas; alternatives are any villa along the canal (e.g. near Mira or Stra).
