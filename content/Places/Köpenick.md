# Köpenick

Old riverside town in south-east [[Berlin]], at the meeting of the Dahme and the Spree a few miles from the Grünau regatta course. In _The Boys in the Boat_ it is the American crew's home during the 1936 Olympics, a quaint, friendly town hiding the persecution the boys cannot see.

## The Boys in the Boat — Daniel James Brown (2013)
Source: [Original](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/310727/the-boys-in-the-boat-by-daniel-james-brown/) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m87k3cnvjndqpnpw1z7q80ag)

> Later that afternoon, the boys arrived at what would be their home for the next several weeks, a police cadet training academy in Köpenick, just a few miles down the Langer See from the Olympic rowing course at Grünau.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** On July 24, 1936, the oarsmen are bused not to the Olympic village but to a brand-new glass, steel and concrete police cadet academy in Köpenick, squeaky-clean but cold, with cold-water showers and police horses stabled on the ground floor. Here Don Hume's cold worsens, the crew lives through the heat and the final, and on the night of the medal Joe lies awake. On his first evening Joe walks past the Rathaus and a synagogue on Freiheit street, and the boys find the town "charmingly quaint". The narrator sets this against what they cannot see: in June 1933 SA men tortured ninety-one of Köpenick's Jews, Social Democrats and Catholics to death; the synagogue at 8 Freiheit would burn on Kristallnacht; and of the Jewish Hirschhahn family, whose clothing shop the boys might have peeked into, the SS would take young Ruth first and send Richard and Hedwig to Auschwitz in 1943, while Eva alone escaped and survived. In 2011 the author takes the S-Bahn out to Köpenick, walks its cobbled streets and passes the vacant lot where the synagogue stood.
- **Why it matters:** Köpenick is the book's clearest picture of the Nazi illusion: a peaceful, welcoming town the boys love, laid over atrocities past and to come. It is also the crew's base for the whole Olympic campaign.
