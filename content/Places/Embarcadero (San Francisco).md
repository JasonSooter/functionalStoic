# Embarcadero (San Francisco)

Waterfront street in [[San Francisco]]; in Jenny Odell's How to Do Nothing her lunchtime refuge there turns out to be the site of the 1934 longshoremen's strike.

## How to Do Nothing — Jenny Odell (2019)
Source: [[How to Do Nothing — Reader's Companion]] · [[How to Do Nothing]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL20078135W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5e31rmq8h3g70rtwm7dmqq5)

> I used to take long lunch breaks as a small, selfish act of resistance. I’d sit on the Embarcadero waterfront, looking plaintively out at the Bay Bridge and the diving ducks.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** The author's lunchtime refuge turns out to be the site of the 1934 longshoremen's strike, where thousands picketed along the Embarcadero, strikers queued for free lunches in block-long lines, and police rode picketers off the sidewalk with horses.
- **Why it matters:** The place links her small private refusal to a massive collective one, and grounds the chapter's account of discipline and the 'third space' in a specific waterfront.

## Walkable City — Jeff Speck (2012)
Source: [[Walkable City]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL36883695M/Walkable_City) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5fbjvzpza8xv239ppmjvbps)

> The Embarcadero was replaced by a lovely boulevard, whose cute little streetcars actually transport more riders per day than the freeway once did.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the book:** Speck cites the Embarcadero Freeway, collapsed in the 1989 earthquake, as an example of traffic simply disappearing after removal. The boulevard cost $171 million but raised property values 300 percent along a 1.2-mile stretch.
- **Why it matters:** A classic American demonstration of reduced demand and of the economics of replacing an elevated expressway with a tree-lined boulevard.
