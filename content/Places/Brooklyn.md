## Dept. of Speculation — Jenny Offill (2014)

Source: [[Dept. Of Speculation.md]] · [Original](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/228424/dept-of-speculation-by-jenny-offill/9780345806871) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5bfwhhsdveh0366hfbxrr5h)

> The daughter cries for Brooklyn.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** The marriage, the baby and the affair all play out here: an apartment with a church across the street, a Rite Aid a block away where the colicky baby is calm under the bright lights, a later apartment on the eleventh floor. In the last sections the family leaves the city for the wife's sister's ramshackle house in the country, and "The daughter cries for Brooklyn."
- **Why it matters:** Brooklyn is the book's home ground, almost never described directly but always there; the move away from it is the novel's last turn. No street or neighbourhood is ever named, so the pin is the borough.

## Four Thousand Weeks — Oliver Burkeman (2021)

Source: [[Four Thousand Weeks]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL22355777W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5fcpy2jkw6b1vgn558c6cty)

> I remember sitting on a park bench near my home in Brooklyn one winter morning in 2014, feeling even more anxious than usual about the volume of undone tasks

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** Burkeman recalls sitting on a park bench near his Brooklyn home one winter morning in 2014, anxious about undone tasks, and realising his productivity systems would never work. He feels immediate relief. Later he refers back to 'that park bench in Brooklyn' as where he began to grasp the efficiency trap.
- **Why it matters:** It is the personal origin moment of the book's thesis, but the text never names the park or street, so only the borough is supportable. Elsewhere the setting could be any bench; Brooklyn adds little beyond biography.
