# Karl-Marx-Allee

Monumental socialist boulevard in former East [[Berlin]], lined with uniform apartment blocks. David Byrne rides it in _Bicycle Diaries_, and Charles Montgomery cites it in _Happy City_; for both it is architecture on a superhuman scale, built to project power and optimism.

## At a glance
| Source | Author | Role | In one line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bicycle Diaries | David Byrne | journey waypoint | Byrne rides the East's grand boulevard to the Stasi Museum, awed by its "not quite human" scale. |

## Bicycle Diaries — David Byrne (2009)
Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL14993423W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m7641s0b4n8x1fe665vraggc)

> I bike out, appropriately enough, along the amazing Karl-Marx-Allee, a sort of Soviet-inspired version of the Champs Élysées or Avenida 9 de Julio in Buenos Aires or maybe New York’s Park Avenue.

- **Role:** journey waypoint
- **In the book:** After reading _Stasiland_, Byrne heads for the Stasi Museum, out in the suburbs and hard to find. The boulevard is wider and grander than its Western models, its repeating apartment blocks a vision of an idealistic, utopian infinity; one side has forlorn ground-floor shops, the other cafés.
- **Why it matters:** The ride sets up the Stasi visit and frames East Berlin through its planning, one of the book's main lenses.

## Happy City — Charles Montgomery (2013)
Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL21093281W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m83dcc1g7eag03hhx6fbc5r3)

> You can still see remnants of that vision on Berlin’s Karl-Marx-Allee.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the book:** In his history of cities as happiness projects, Montgomery points to the boulevard as a remnant of the socialist-realist rebuilding of postwar Eastern Europe, designed to exude power, optimism and public elegance. It is almost 300 feet wide, so it feels empty without a military parade, and its offices and once-elegant workers' apartments earned the nickname "wedding cake".
- **Why it matters:** An example of a state imposing its vision of the good life from above through architecture, which the book contrasts with happiness built from how people actually live.
