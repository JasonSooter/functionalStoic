# Wu-shan (Prince Jen)

An enchanted mountain in Lloyd Alexander's fairy-tale China, pinned at the real Wu Mountains on the Yangtze in [[Chongqing]], whose name it shares. In the novel it is where General Li Kwang and his whole escort are turned to stone.

## The Remarkable Journey of Prince Jen — Lloyd Alexander (1991)
Source: [Original](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/319277/the-remarkable-journey-of-prince-jen-by-lloyd-alexander/9780142402252) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m887mn915dracg1eajv8ksv4)

> You are in Wu-shan, Mountain of Sorcerers.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** Left waiting by the road, Li Kwang mends and tries the old saddle Master Wu chose as a gift. His mare bolts off the road toward high, bare grey peaks and into a cavern whose stone wall splits open on a hidden green land. There the Lady of Fearful Awakenings puts him to sleep, and he wakes turned to stone with all his troops; Master Wu appears and tells him where he is.
- **Why it matters:** Losing the escort here strips Jen of his soldiers, so he continues with only his servant Mafoo and the flute girl Voyaging Moon. That turns a royal progress into the humbling journey the book is about.
- **Real vs. fictional:** The mountain is magical and its geography is not literal.
- **Where is it?:** ⚠️ Best guess. The text gives only weak clues: high grey peaks that blaze red at sunset, a day's fast ride off the road, an enchanted country inside. No review or reference work places it. The name matches Wushan ("Witch Mountain"), the real Wu Mountains of the Three Gorges, so the pin is at Wushan County. That is a naming echo, not geography: the real range lies far south of the book's other borrowed names (Ch'angan, the Lo, the T'ung Pass). Alternative: any mountain near Xi'an, or no real model at all.
