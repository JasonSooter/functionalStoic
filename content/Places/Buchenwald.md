# Buchenwald

Nazi concentration camp near Weimar, Germany. A journey waypoint in Colum McCann's _Apeirogon_: Rami and Bassam walk through the camp gates on their German tour; Rami reads the inscription from inside.

## Apeirogon — Colum McCann (2020)

Source: [[Apeirogon]] · [Original](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/207433/apeirogon-a-novel-by-colum-mccann/) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5jjqht6s2taazaqw3atjfnx)

> They traveled south to Leipzig and walked through the gates at Buchenwald. It felt to Rami like an ancient ruin.

- **Role:** journey waypoint
- **In the book:** On the German speaking tour, Rami and Bassam walk through the gates at Buchenwald. Rami reads the inscription Jedem das Seine, which can only be read from the inside, and they walk out together.
- **Why it matters:** A moment where Rami, who had vowed never to visit Germany, stands with a Palestinian companion in the camp he had studied since childhood. It is brief but pivotal in his arc.

## Germany: Memories of a Nation — Neil MacGregor (2014)

Source: [[Germany - Memories of a Nation]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL20201162W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5m76wvkt3xq1vp6adr778vs)

> It is not enough merely to look into this camp. One has to go in, through this gate, and then look out in order to read the motto, boldly lettered in iron, which every prisoner who arrived at Buchenwald read as they looked back at the world from which they were being removed:

- **Role:** subject
- **In the book:** Chapter 25, 'At the Buchenwald gate', is built around the entrance gate and its motto Jedem das Seine, lettered in Bauhaus style by the prisoner Franz Ehrlich and read by every prisoner looking back at the world. The camp lies on the Ettersberg, where Goethe walked, a few miles from Weimar. The chapter follows liberation by the Americans in 1945, the Soviet use of the camp afterwards and the divided memorial politics of East and West.
- **Why it matters:** It is the book's focal point for how Germany's humane traditions could collapse into the camps, with Weimar and Buchenwald side by side.
