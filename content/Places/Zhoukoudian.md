# Zhoukoudian

Peking Man site on Dragon Bone Hill, south-west of Beijing. Nature's piece on Chinese human origins opens there, and _A Short History of Nearly Everything_ tells how its Peking Man bones were found and then lost.

## How China is rewriting the book on human origins — Nature (2016)
Source: [Original](http://www.nature.com/news/how-china-is-rewriting-the-book-on-human-origins-1.20231) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5jx736var2sksb2t79fbnyw)

> On the outskirts of Beijing, a small limestone mountain named Dragon Bone Hill rises above the surrounding sprawl. Along the northern side, a path leads up to some fenced-off caves that draw 150,000 visitors each year, from schoolchildren to grey-haired pensioners. It was here, in 1929, that researchers discovered a nearly complete ancient skull that they determined was roughly half a million years old.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the article:** Opens the article at the cave site where Peking Man was found, which launched the idea that humans first evolved in Asia; its significance later faded against African finds but still haunts Chinese researchers.
- **Why it matters:** Anchor of the whole narrative about China's role in human origins.

## A Short History of Nearly Everything — Bill Bryson (2003)
Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL74128W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m8332wfxxw58w3wcyfxdq8xz)

> a place, Dragon Bone Hill, that was locally famous as a hunting ground for old bones

- **Role:** subject
- **In the book:** Davidson Black searched Dragon Bone Hill, where locals dug up old bones to grind into medicine, and from a single molar announced a new species, Sinanthropus pekinensis, Peking Man. Later digging turned up more bones, but all were lost the day after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941, when U.S. Marines tried to spirit them out of the country.
- **Why it matters:** A site of both discovery and loss, illustrating how fragile and fragmentary the hominid record is.
