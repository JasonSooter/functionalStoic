# Saint Petersburg

Russia's second city, on the Neva. In Mark Manson's book a month there in 2011 teaches him the value of Russian bluntness.

## The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck — Mark Manson (2016)
Source: [[The Subtle Art of Not Giving a Fck]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL17590212W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5kvb6323d29478shq0b4m1c)

> In 2011, I traveled to Saint Petersburg, Russia. The food sucked. The weather sucked.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** In 2011 Manson spends a month in Saint Petersburg, disliking the food and weather but loving it. A coffee date tells him flatly that something he said was stupid; he comes to value the bluntness as honest, unconditional expression, and his Russian teacher explains it as trust being the key currency under communism.
- **Why it matters:** It is his example of how travel exposes you to other values, and of what Western politeness costs.

## Co-Intelligence — Ethan Mollick (2024)
Source: [[Co-Intelligence]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL37565105W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5m771bj1n17q3mcpx5z0rfv)

> In 2001, in Saint Petersburg, three programmers, Vladimir Veselov, Eugene Demchenko, and Sergey Ulasen, developed perhaps the first chatbot to have a claim to passing the Turing Test.

- **Role:** backdrop
- **In the book:** In 2001 three programmers in Saint Petersburg build Eugene Goostman, a chatbot posing as a thirteen-year-old Ukrainian boy so that judges forgive its errors. In 2014 a third of the judges at a contest take it for human after five minutes, though most researchers say it passed through loopholes.
- **Why it matters:** A minor stop in the chapter on imitation games, showing how early chatbots gamed the Turing Test with persona tricks.
