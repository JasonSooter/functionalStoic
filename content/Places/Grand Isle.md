# Grand Isle

Barrier island on the Gulf coast of [[Louisiana]], USA. It is where Kate Chopin's The Awakening begins, in a summer colony, and where Edna swims out into the Gulf at the end.

## The Awakening — Kate Chopin (1899)

Source: [[The Awakening]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL65430W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5crcp05ndz9ychepk1azz07)

> He was spending his summer vacation, as he always did, with his mother at Grand Isle.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** The first part of the novel takes place on Grand Isle: Madame Lebrun's cottages and main house, the beach and bathhouses, the Gulf. Edna learns to swim, is moved by Mademoiselle Reisz's playing and falls for Robert there. In the last chapter she returns alone out of season, finds Victor and Mariequita repairing the Lebrun house, walks down past the old bathhouse to the empty beach, and swims out into the Gulf until her strength is gone; the novel ends with her memories as she swims.
- **Why it matters:** Edna's awakening begins here and the novel ends here; the sea and the freedoms of the Creole summer colony drive the plot.
- **Real vs. fictional:** Real island, named in the text; the Lebrun pension and cottages are fictional.

## Great Short Books — Kenneth C. Davis (2022)
Source: [[Great Short Books]] · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m8f01g8r2wvxc5j2dp2c9hq0)

> The Awakening opens as twenty-eight-year-old Edna Pontellier summers with her two small boys on Grand Island, a seaside resort in the Gulf of Mexico.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** Davis's entry on _The Awakening_ opens his "Dangerous Beach Holidays": Edna summers on the island with her two boys while her husband leaves for business and card games. When Robert Lebrun, a young man staying on the island, teaches her to swim, Davis calls it "a liberating act", and he quotes Chopin as she dips her toes into the Gulf: "The voice of the sea is seductive;… the touch of the sea is sensuous." He adds that the atmosphere of 1890s New Orleans, where she returns, is also key to the novel.
- **Why it matters:** Davis reads the island as the place where the sea itself awakens Edna, the reason he files the book with other seaside novels where "bad things can happen".
- **Real vs. fictional:** Davis writes "Grand Island"; it is the novel's Grand Isle.
