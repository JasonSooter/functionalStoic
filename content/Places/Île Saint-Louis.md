# Île Saint-Louis

The smaller of the two natural islands in the Seine in central [[Paris]], just upstream of the Île de la Cité and Notre-Dame. It is the spot in Bill Bryson's Paris chapter that he loves without reservation.

## Neither Here Nor There — Bill Bryson (1991)
Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL74125W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5a831z3zh1s735kg7xz6ezd)

> There facing me was the Île St.-Louis, glowing softly and floating on the river like a vision, a medieval hamlet magically preserved in the midst of a modern city.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** On his first March evening in Paris, Bryson walks to the Seine, reaches it at the Pont de Sully, and crosses onto the island. He wanders its "half dozen shuttered streets," glancing up at lit, book-lined apartments and envying the people at the western end whose windows look onto Notre-Dame. Then he circles the cathedral and watches the bateaux-mouches.
- **Why it matters:** It's the one spot in his Paris chapter that he loves without reservation, and it balances the rants about traffic and architecture that follow. The island shows the human-scale city he later says planners have forgotten.
