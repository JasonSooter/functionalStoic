# Magdalen College, Oxford

College of the University of Oxford, on the High Street by the Cherwell. In Alan Jacobs's _The Pleasures of Reading in an Age of Distraction_ it is where the young Edward Gibbon idled through his "most idle and unprofitable" months, the book's cautionary case of reading by unguided whim.

## The Pleasures of Reading in an Age of Distraction — Alan Jacobs (2011)
Source: [[The Pleasures of Reading in an Age of Distraction]] · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m8enny6jrmg4ytjb743jv4kw)

> He read almost nothing while he was resident at Magdalen, chiefly because he had a strong sense of confinement in that (to him) monastic space, and since his “chief pleasure was that of travelling,” he got out of town whenever possible.

- **Role:** backdrop
- **In the book:** In the chapter on Whim, Jacobs tells how Gibbon was sent to Oxford at fourteen, in the mid-eighteenth century, when the university had "virtually forgotten its intellectual and pedagogical calling". Gibbon called his time at Magdalen "the most idle and unprofitable of my whole life": his tutors, the "monks", were supine, he skipped tutorials unchecked, and he read almost nothing while in residence. Idleness led him toward Catholicism, and his father removed him to the care of a Swiss Protestant, under whom he finally began to study seriously.
- **Why it matters:** Magdalen is Jacobs's picture of lowercase whim: freedom without direction, which made Gibbon miserable rather than happy. The contrast with the Swiss tutor frames the book's guiding question of how to move from "blind propensity" to informed Whim without a teacher. Any lax institution would serve the argument, but Gibbon's own words about Magdalen give it its force.
