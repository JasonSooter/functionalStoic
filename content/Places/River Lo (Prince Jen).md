# River Lo (Prince Jen)

The broad river Prince Jen must cross to reach T'ien-kuo in Lloyd Alexander's fairy-tale China. It is pinned at the real Luo River in [[Henan]], which, like the book's Lo, lies beyond the T'ung Pass. A storm capsizes the ferry here and scatters the travellers.

## The Remarkable Journey of Prince Jen — Lloyd Alexander (1991)
Source: [Original](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/319277/the-remarkable-journey-of-prince-jen-by-lloyd-alexander/9780142402252) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m887mppppd3t0zk2j6qsg4hh)

> The wind had freshened; the waters of the Lo turned choppy. Well before the boat reached midstream, the waves began snatching at it.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** The poet Master Shu, freed from the cangue, says "T'ien-kuo lies beyond the River Lo" and guides the party to a ferryman's hut upriver of Chen-yeh, "the district capital close to the banks of the Lo". The river is so broad Jen can barely see the far shore. He insists on crossing despite the threat of a storm; the boat overturns, everyone is scattered, and Jen wakes in a fisherman's hut far downstream. Voyaging Moon, meanwhile, ends up playing the flute at the Golden Grasshopper inn in Chen-yeh and is sold south. Jen walks back upstream to Chen-yeh, learns of her kidnapping, and turns south after her, giving up his mission.
- **Why it matters:** The river is the hinge of the book. It separates the lovers and forces Jen to choose between his embassy to T'ien-kuo and Voyaging Moon. Any barrier would have split them, but the river's sheer width and the storm make the separation feel final.
- **Real vs. fictional:** Chen-yeh, the ferry and the Golden Grasshopper inn are fictional.
- **Where is it?:** ⚠️ Inferred. Clues in the text: the river lies beyond the T'ung Pass, "less than a week of easy travel" from it (144406), and is very wide. In real geography the Luo River lies east of the Tong Pass (Tongguan), on the road from Chang'an toward Luoyang, so the book's three borrowed names (Ch'angan, T'ung Pass, Lo) line up. No reference work confirms the model. The pin is the Wikipedia point for the Luo, near its mouth on the Yellow River; the book's crossing is unlocated. Alternatives: the Yellow River itself, or a purely invented river.
