# Casa Azul

Frida Kahlo's blue house on the corner of Londres and Allende streets in [[Coyoacán]], Mexico City, now the Museo Frida Kahlo. Hayden Herrera's biography of Kahlo opens here, in the house where her story begins and ends.

## Frida — Hayden Herrera (1983)
Source: [[Frida - A Biography of Frida Kahlo]] · [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4114749W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m72js8sb34ehs62c1tqzdhar)

> From the outside, the house on the corner of Londres and Allende streets looks very like other houses in Coyoacán, an old residential section on the southwestern periphery of Mexico City.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** Chapter 1, "The Blue House on Londres Street", opens with the line that Frida Kahlo's story "begins and ends in the same place". Herrera walks the reader through the one-story house with bright blue walls, built by her father Guillermo in 1904 and turned into a museum holding her paintings and belongings. She notes that the inscriptions on its walls about Frida's birth and her marriage embroider the truth. In a diary entry, Frida recalls breathing on the window of her childhood room, which gave onto Allende Street, and drawing a door through which she escaped in her imagination to the Pinzón dairy and an imaginary friend.
- **Why it matters:** The house frames the whole biography: Herrera starts and ends Frida's life in it, and it is the home she returns to. Set anywhere else, the book would lose its anchoring image.

- **Later in the book:** In her last year, after the amputation of her leg, Frida paints again in her studio here from her wheelchair. In June 1954 she has her four-poster bed moved from her small corner bedroom into the passageway leading to the studio, and she dies in the house on 13 July 1954, as the opening chapter promised.

- **Coda:** The book closes here. Rivera gives the house, its art collection and furnishings to the Mexican people in 1955, keeping "a corner" for himself; when the Frida Kahlo Museum opens in July 1958 her ashes lie in a sack on her bed under her death mask. Herrera ends with a walk through the rooms (the wheelchair before the easel, a painted plaster corset on the bed, the Chinese dolls) and with her last painting, the watermelons inscribed "VIVA LA VIDA", hanging in the living room: "Because she was an invalid, the house in Coyoacán became her world."
