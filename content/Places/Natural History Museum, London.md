# Natural History Museum, London

Museum in South Kensington, London. In _Life on Air_ it is home of the fossil named Attenborosaurus; in _A Short History of Nearly Everything_ it is Richard Owen's creation and the place Bryson tours behind the scenes with Richard Fortey.

## Life on Air — David Attenborough (2002)
Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL24547776M/Life_on_air) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5hy8mkm3qzg45gdef3gj32k)

> I asked Angela where this remarkable object could be seen. It was still on display, hanging on the wall of a gallery a few yards away from where we were talking.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** In a gallery, Angela Milner congratulates him on Attenborosaurus; the cast still carries the old label because the redecoration budget was spent.
- **Why it matters:** A gentle anecdote; the museum is the site of the joke about his fossil fame.

## A Short History of Nearly Everything — Bill Bryson (2003)

Source: [Original](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL74128W) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m82rpb961k7p6h5jqxsw7f0p)

> Today his statue commands a masterly view from the staircase of the main hall in the Natural History Museum, while Darwin and T. H. Huxley are consigned somewhat obscurely to the museum coffee shop, where they stare gravely over people snacking on cups of tea and jam doughnuts.

> The spirit room alone holds fifteen miles of shelving containing jar upon jar of animals preserved in methylated spirit.

- **Role:** setting
- **In the book:** Bryson credits Richard Owen with creating the museum, a "grand and beloved Gothic heap" opened in 1880, and with insisting it be open to everyone, including working people in the evenings. Owen's statue now looks down from the main staircase, while Darwin and Huxley are consigned to the coffee shop. Later, on a gray February day, Bryson meets the paleontologist Richard Fortey here and is shown cupboards of drawers holding twenty thousand trilobites, the starting point for the Burgess Shale and Cambrian-explosion debates. In Chapter 23 Fortey takes him through the secret doors behind the public galleries: about 70 million objects, fifteen miles of shelving in the spirit room alone, tales of the specimen thief Richard Meinertzhagen, and finally the botany department, where Len Ellis keeps 780,000 moss specimens in mahogany cabinets built for Joseph Banks's Endeavour collections.
- **Why it matters:** The museum is Bryson's base for the chapters on fossils and on how much life remains uncatalogued; its hidden collections and lone specialists are the chapter's argument made physical.
