# St. Louis

City on the Mississippi in eastern Missouri. In _Stoner_ it is the home of Edith Bostwick's banker parents, where Stoner asks for her hand, spends a failed honeymoon, and where her father's bank collapses in 1929.

## Stoner — John Williams (1965)

Source: [Original](https://www.nyrb.com/products/stoner) · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m8afpbx76wr7nk8r0kn5a25g)

> It was the largest and most elegant house that Stoner had ever been in.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the book:** Edith "lived with her parents in St. Louis", where her father is president of one of the smaller banks. Stoner takes the train down to meet them. Their house is high-ceilinged and dark, crowded with vases, silver on marble-topped tables and furniture so fragile he is afraid to move. After a stiff talk about his "prospects", Horace and Mrs. Bostwick consent; Edith insists "it must be soon," then avoids him all day. That night in the guest room he cannot sleep and, for the first time, questions what he is about to do, before catching the early train back to Columbia. After the wedding the couple honeymoon in the city: he half carries Edith "through the entrance of the Ambassador Hotel, a massive structure of brown cut stone," and spends the wedding night on the sofa of their tenth-floor suite, which looks out to where "the Mississippi River wound its bluish-brown length in the morning sun." The next day, at the Art Museum, he feels a real tenderness for her, before a second night ends with Edith sick in the bathroom. In October 1929 "a small private bank, the Merchant’s Trust, in St. Louis", which holds the mortgage on the Stoners' house and of which Edith's father is president, fails; three days later Horace Bostwick shoots himself in his office there. Edith stays on after the funeral "in her old room, the room in which she had grown up," and comes back to Columbia changed.
- **Why it matters:** The engagement and honeymoon scenes set out the cold marriage that dominates the middle of the novel, and the house's wealth and stiffness show the gulf between Edith's world and Stoner's farm. The father's ruin in the city is the turning point that frees Edith to begin her long campaign against her husband.
- **Real vs. fictional:** The city is real; the Merchant's Trust is the novel's invention, and the text gives no address for the Bostwick house or the hotel.
