## Why swimming in the Rhine is allowed in Switzerland but banned in Germany — Franziska Müller, Simon Ormiston (2026)
Source: [[Why Swimming in the Rhine Is Allowed in Switzerland but Banned in Germany]] · [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5beyjygrdhbx1d1r2q0fr2y)

> It is 32C in Basel. On the bridges, people are eating ice cream while below them swimmers drift downstream in the Rhine, sharing the river with freight vessels and pleasure boats.

- **Role:** subject
- **In the article:** About 4,500 people take part in Basel's annual Rhine swim, about two kilometres downstream from Kleinbasel to the Johanniter Bridge. The city has built showers, steps, entry points and rescue plans, and publishes maps of safe spots with the Swiss Life Saving Society. A 1986 chemical-warehouse fire that poisoned the river prompted decades of work to clean it. A few hundred metres downstream, in France, and across the river in Germany, swimming is banned; Cologne and Düsseldorf have prohibited it since 2025, and Berlin's Flussbad campaigners hold Basel up as the model.
- **Why it matters:** Basel is the article's proof that river swimming can be managed; without it, the piece would only be a list of bans.

## The Rhine River explained in under 3 Minutes — FactSpark (2026)
Source: [Readwise highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m5bfx6hwf0bg39nn12187knm)

> the Rhine turns sharply at Basel's "Rhine knee," passing the Switzerland-France-Germany tripoint border.

- **Role:** turning point
- **In the video:** A quick tour down the river. At the end of its Swiss stretch the Rhine bends sharply at Basel; the narrow, fast stretch below hindered navigation, so France built the Grand Canal d'Alsace alongside it for shipping and hydropower.
- **Why it matters:** The bend is where the river turns from an east–west border river into the north-flowing Upper Rhine; a minor place in a three-minute survey, but a cross-reference to the swimming article.
